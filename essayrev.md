---
title: The Silent Truths of Tourism in Mexico
author: Miguel Corral
---

In Mexico, there was a mass initiative to bring in more tourists to help the country make much-needed money.
For a long time, Mexico has been heavily promoted for its beautiful beaches, exotic scenery, and rich culture, which seems like the perfect vacation for families from across the globe to indulge in.
While it may seem that this is a massive opportunity for the economy and for the people of the region to make their own money, the actual locals and indigenous people who need the land and money to survive are hardly making anything while the businessmen are capitalizing on the opportunity to invest into tourism and making money for themselves.
The pre-existing local and indigenous people of Mexico have lived in the land that has been industrialized and commercialized for monetary gain, not to their exact benefit and they have no power to change it.
The surrounding region of select tourist districts promotes the deforestation of mangroves and the destruction of land to make way for hotels, resorts, and various other uses for the land, and the abuse of ancient ruins to become monetized parks.
There is also gentrification in the major tourist districts making the area expensive and inaccessible to locals, pushing them out of the area and into poverty and insufficient living.
This has caused a skew of the culture and social life of the locals that was once genuine and flourishing.

Tourism is a crucial part of a country's economy, there is no difference in the case of Mexico.
'History.com' reveals that Quintano Roo gained statehood in the early 1970s, during that same period, there was a mass push for tourism in areas like Cancún, Isla Mujeres, and Cozumel which began to develop as tourist destinations ("Quintana Roo, 2018).
It was revealed from Linda M. Ambrosie in the book 'Annals of Tourism Research' that the initial investment of the Cancun project from multiple parties received "The combined initial investment..MXP 547.5 million" (Ambrosie, 76, 2015).
On paper, this selection seemed perfect for many reasons, open untouched land to work on, beautiful sinkhole cenotes, glistening beaches, and ancient Mayan ruins inside all one state waiting to be capitalized on.
FONUTAR (Fondo Nacional de Fomento al Turismo/The National Fund for the Promotion of Tourist) and other government-based institutions were the massive bearers that pushed and enacted the tourism change in Quintana Roo.
By the 1990's it became a tourist hotspot for people all over the globe not only being a hotspot in Mexico, but a hotspot for all of Latin America.
This is backed up by Michael Clancy in the book 'MEXICAN TOURISM: Export Growth and Structural Change since 1970' as he displays, \"By the 1990s, the country ranked first in arrivals in Latin America,drawing nearly 40 percent of all international travelers to the region"(Clancy, 130, 2001).
Now, mass tourism extends from Cancun to the south to Tulum where a dedicated tourist district resides on the coast of Quintana Roo with luxury hotels, resorts, beaches, ecological theme parks, underground rivers, and nature trails in which the name 'Riviera Maya' was coined.
Donald Wood in "Quintana Roo\'s Tourism Numbers Bounce Back to Near Pre-Pandemic Levels" announced that Quintana Roo as of 2021 received "\...12.5 million visitors..." (Wood, 2022), and these are numbers recently after the outburst of the Covid-19 pandemic making the attraction of people extremely impressive.
There is no doubt that this initiative has brought an influx of money and noise to Mexico making it something to consider, but there is an uncontrolled continuation and exploitation of what has been created and offered which has led to frustrating circumstances.

Before the reign of mass tourism invading Quintana Roo, the state was mostly left to the indigenous communities scattered over the Yucatan Peninsula.
Those people who resided in the area before the drastic changes of industrialization were various Mayan communities that harbored their own unique culture compared to the northern parts of Mexico with origins more in line with the Aztecs.
The mass tourism push greatly changed the Maya people inhabiting the land with pace and in abundance.
Pi-Sunyer Oriol and Thomas R. Brooke, authors in the book 'The Journal of Latin American and Caribbean Anthropology' say that " indigenous people, who generally lived in the interior, were increasingly both pulled and pushed into this tourism-based labor market" (Pi-Sunyer and Thomas, 94, 2015).
The quote is about the Maya living in the Riviera Maya, painting a picture of how they had to conform to a radical change of daily life and resort to falling in and out of the new economic, political, and social practices that were alien and extremely modern for any community to be thrown into.
Andi Sarmiento, in the article "Mayapolis: gentrification in the Riviera Maya," addresses one of many current problems of the usage of Maya people and their name saying, "By putting the word Mayan on a product, it automatically becomes a tourist attraction and therefore, its value increases" (Sarmiento, 2023).
The Maya people are being exploited by businesses that bank on the people who do not have the power to say they cannot do that.
They also explain that the Maya "are simply treated as marketing strategy rather than the people they are" (Sarmiento, 2023).
What is also interesting to consider is that these people unfortunately need to fall into the character of a "traditional" Maya person so that they can make a scrap of money to please the tourists.
The people need to do all they can to provide for themselves and their families.
It is blatant that when placed in a situation like this, it forces people to adapt to survive and create a living however they can.
Unfortunately, businesses will continue to abuse the aspect of the Mayan culture to their liking and their envisionment of a culture, and the entertainment of others.
Another great input is from Juan Carlos Guerra Perez from the post "The gentrification of the Riviera Maya" (English Translated), in which Perez goes on to say, "Those who remain do so marginalized within the system, either as temporary workers or simple laborers" (Perez, 2019).
Again, they are forced to be set into a lifestyle that was never their intention to fulfill, life for them wasn't to be casual workers of booming businesses to only receive the short end of the stick, within their land.
The system stated earlier refers to the structure of the tourism campaign in the Riviera Maya which can be applied to Quintana Roo as a whole, in which instills a deliberate reach for money, and money in abundance.
So when the people are "marginalized within the system" they are seen as peons when it comes to the plan to create a social, political, and economic structure, almost completely forgotten unless it's for the embodiment of the "spirit" of their culture.

Mexico is considered to have some of the most beautiful and diverse landscapes within the various regions of the country.
Spacious deserts, tangled forests, warm beaches all are extremely rich in diversity and distinct in their own ways.
This goes to say that the landscape of Mexico is a part of the cultural identity of the country and the factor of the lifestyles of the locals that have adapted due to their regional circumstances.
Unfortunately if there needs to be room to open more residencies, extensive resorts and hotels in the case of mass tourism, there needs to be massive land changes to fit these buildings.
In the case of the Quitnana Roo which hold mangrove forests near the coasts of the state and all around the Yucatan Peninsula, there has been mass deforestation of one of the most interesting benefactors to the region\'s ecosystem.
Madelin Andersen in 'LOSS OF MEXICO'S VALUABLE MANGROVE FORESTS' actively defines mangroves as "\...marine trees that form coastal wetlands known as mangrove forests and can be found around the world in tropical and sub-tropical climates.
Mangroves have evolved to survive in coastal wetland environments where the salt and oxygen poor soil makes it impossible for other plants to live" (Andersen, 2018).
Looking into the last part explaining the importance the mangroves are to the ecosystem considering the landscape, it puts into perspective the importance of preserving them. Writers David Vargas-del-Río and Ludger Brenner, in the book 'Ocean & Coastal Management' bring up an important finding from their research when they state, "Business and urbanization remove mangroves, fill in the wetlands, convert lagoons into marinas, and urbanize the coastal dune (CS 2.1, 5). Roads interrupt natural water flows, causing a progressive deterioration of the mangroves (CS 2.1, 5)" (Vargas del Rio, Brenner, 2023).
This research is in context to how entrepreneurs in Mexico handle the climate in which they make money off the land.
They can either touristify the mangroves themselves as they are remarkable emblems of nature, or in order to create other business ventures, disrupt the land to build whatever they will, inherently messing with the ecosystem of the mangroves.
With both pieces of information in mind, it's understandable that looking to conserve such important pieces of nature for the surrounding environment would be a top priority, but because of the greed for money ligers within entrepreneurs minds, they will sacrifice what makes the land special to take part in a rampant venture of what makes money in the tourist infested region.
The sustainability of the ancient ruins is important as they are relics of the past for historians looking to uncover more about the world, Mayans who have a cultural connection to the ruins of their ancestors, and tourists who genuinely want to understand and learn the importance of the but also take in the visual beauty.
What is more debatable is the way tourists can interact with the ancient architecture and culture.
They have to go through the process of entering an archaeological park in which companies have commercialized the ruins for monetary gain.
Cameron Walker in the book 'Tourism and Applied Anthropologists', provides an example of Chichen Itza which is an archeological site within Yucatan, one of the many Mayan creations.
Walker states, "When a major archaeological park such as Chichen Itza has been excavated, reconstructed, and successfully promoted as a majortourism destination, it is usually marketed as a commodity, which externalizes the site.
As such, it may come to be seen as lacking in authenticity, even though the buildings are authentic in the archaeological sense" (Waler, 72, 2005).
The problem that Walker brings up isn't the definition of what is authentic or not, it's the gray area of commodifying the site to be promoted as a major tourist region and what makes it preservation and restoration or using the site for commercial gain.
This brings up that there is a tricky balance that needs to be maintained to have a proper representation of a long running culture while not running it dry with the objective of making significant profit instead.

'National Geographic' loosely describes gentrification as a socioeconomic process in which a community receives massive change not only in the visual aspect but also in the character aspect of the community (National Geographic Society, 2023).
This shift can only really be done by others who are affluent and in power to influence change.
This is done for the opportunity to invest in the area which will slowly affect the surrounding neighborhoods to conform to the pressures of gentrification.
This is a general problem that reaches all across the world, with the likes of big cities like Chicago.
This is very relevant to the people of Quintana Roo and the Riviera Maya with the bustling business of tourism as its influence reaches far past money but the shift of culture in these hotspots.
Also, with the extreme supply and demand of taking part in the wonderland of Mexico there is a price, and simply put, the Maya people cannot live in their land because of the inflation of prices in their communities.
To begin to divulge into this, it would be smart to propose a vastly important statistic that comes from Mattha Busby in the article 'Mexican tourism boom: Tulum's locals fight evictions as developers move in' which states, "Tensions are brewing in step with rapid gentrification and social change, which has seen the poverty rate jump to 62% -- the highest in the country" (Busby, 2022).
To clear things up more, Isabella Cota in 'The poverty that Tulum's tourism boom cannot hide' adds more context by saying, "The rate went from 32% in 2015 to 62% in 2020" (Cota, 2022).
With these quotes in mind, it is polarizing to comprehend that there is a change like this, it's truly alarming to hear numbers this vast for any place.
The majority within the Riviera Maya cannot afford to live in the area anymore, likely to the area adhering to the more financially comfortable to take advantage of those that have the money to spend and spend it generously.
There is no remorse for the ones that don't fit into that group, and they will be left to fend for themselves or fight to assimilate into an unnecessary way of living, subjects of tourism aiding tourism.
Something also important is when Aljazeera states in the article 'Mexico apologises to Indigenous Maya people for historic abuses' describes the state of the Maya people by explaining "While the coast south of Cancun is known as the "Riviera Maya," and aquatic parks often have "Mayan" attractions, the vast majority of Mayans live in poverty in the southern, undeveloped part of Quintana Roo"(Aljazeera, 2021).
The Maya and local people primarily lived off the land of its resources so when a chunk of land is then distinguished as a tourism hotspot, they have to find another area to fill their needs.
It's also a money problem for the locals. 
The Riviera Maya is expensive because it is fundamentally a money vacuum for incoming visitors to spend money on money-hungry businesses, taking advantage of the oblivious consumers.
Tourism singlehandedly displaced the population of the Mayans and singled out the less fortunate locals who didn't have the resources to create a monopoly within a region that never was set up to do so, until now.
When their living area isn't accustomed to the target audience there is nowhere else to go besides leaving.
Besides the absurd rise in the cost of living in Quintana Roo, what is also fascinating is the cost of commodities and food, fundamental resources of human life.
Thomas L. Leatherman and Alan Goodman, both authors of the book 'Social Science & Medicine' offer interviews with locals of an ancient Maya city in Mexico named Coba.
In one of those interviews, it is revealed," As one resident of Coba \[an ancient Maya city in Mexico\] noted, "There are more foods available now, but no money to buy them" (Leatherman, Goodman, 833, 2005).
This short and simple description of life in Coba is enough to understand the living situation of the people is unacceptable and distinguishes the relationship between the people and the paradoxical nature that there is food in abundance, but there is no way of obtaining it.
The initial idea that food and business will be given to the Mayan people because of tourism and a booming business is swindled because it is clear that the people who need basic human survival needs, aren't taking part of the good half of tourism, only getting pushed away and hidden, merely just a trade-off for the enthralling financial takings in the eyes of the rich.

This singular circumstance can be related to numerous other situations like the native tribes of the United States, pushed and pulled.
This broken record of the rich getting richer and the poor getting poorer, the unfortunate continuing to stay helpless with no way to turn the tide.
The need for power and money vanquishes culture-rich communities that cause no bother.
The outlier between these circumstances is that in the U.S. the natives were ridiculed, and unfortunately forcibly stripped of their roots of culture while in the case of the Maya, their culture is at the forefront, but extremely superficial and forced to light the facade of the ancient and mysterious feel of the ancient Maya people, not the Maya people of the now.
The people can only conform to survive or fall righteously fighting for their culture.
This is an outcry for not only the land and the money, it's about the culture that holds the history that can only truly be preserved through people.

A philosophy I learned that I take to heart is about traveling and visiting foreign places.
This philosophy instills the idea that to have a genuine and rich experience one must place oneself with the local people who have pure connections to the land, culture, and other people to create relationships that provide the actual life.
There is the opportunity for learning and understanding, but it also paves a way to find the niche you would never uncover if you were to do something done by so many others.
I applied this philosophy when I took a trip to Merida, Mexico, a city in Yucatan, which neighbors Quintana Roo directly west.
Merida is critically acclaimed for being the safest city in all of Mexico offering travelers the relief that they can still interact with what Mexico has to offer without the vicious dangerous life average Mexicans live.
As you may expect, tourism infiltrates the main strips and promotes the same Maya culture that runs through the Yucatan Peninsula that Quintano Roo shares with Yucatan.
I avoided tours of the cenotes and temples and simply took walks around the neighborhoods.
Taking in what it had to offer as it came naturally, it made it more intimate, real, and charming that I stumbled upon things under my volition and the lures didn't persuade me of landmark tourism.
It's always so hard to remember that people live in these places as actively working citizens, while you may be visiting for vacation, those people are concerned about their own lives so appreciate their cultures in a fashion that isn't forced and try to directly support them since they aren't in the scope of those who want a superficial paradise.
This isn't to say that experiencing wonders like the ancient Mayan temples should not be done, but incorporating a broader sense of the place you visit will make your overall experience that is authentic and truthful.

The purpose of the research was to attempt to uncover the unspoken problems of tourism in Mexico, how the indigenous people have become involved in the cultural, economic, and social changes of mass tourism, how they were treated as a people, and the outcomes of their lives, land, and culture.
What has been uncovered is that tourism in this case, ends up pushing them away and even exploiting conceived notions about those groups to businesses\' benefit.
Mass tourism has shifted the way of life of people who weren't harboring being an attraction for others.
These people have needed to adapt to these drastic changes to make a living and to have some success in circumstances they have no control over.
The Mayans and locals of Quintana Roo, Mexico, and the Riviera Maya, face similar challenges to others, but the uniqueness of this case is the surprisingly deliberately designed plan to make this tourist strip as perfect as possible for a massive gain economically and socially.
flipping the lives of Mayan people, unaware of the hurdles they would have to cross.
